Which cards win games on their own, and do they justify a color commitment or a splash? Name the pool's strongest cards, say which are build-arounds, and say what it costs to play each one: its colors, and whether a splash can support it.

Your number table holds the grades of the pool's candidate cards, which are its rare and mythic cards that have a grade and any card graded highly, and, for each color pair, how many splash candidates it has. Your facts list the candidates, best grade first, with their rarity and colors, and then, for each pair that has them, the cards that could be splashed, the one color each needs and why it is worth it.

Weigh a card's grade against what it costs. A strong card in one color asks for little; a strong card in two colors asks for that exact pair; a strong card in three colors is a splash at best. Compare the cost with the depth of the colors it needs: a bomb in a thin color may not be worth moving into it, while a bomb in a deep color is a reason to stay. A build-around is a card whose grade depends on other cards the pool must also hold, so check that the pool holds them.

Findings a player acts on:
- The cards that should decide the colors of the deck, and the pair they point to.
- A bomb that is not worth its colors, and why.
- A card that a splash can support, naming the color and whether the pool has fixing for it.
- A build-around, with the cards in the pool that it needs, or a note that they are missing.

Say plainly when the pool has no card that wins on its own.
